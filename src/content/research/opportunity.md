# Research sheet: Opportunity and the 2018 global dust storm
Confidence: A = NASA/JPL statement, B = reputable report quoting NASA/JPL, C = verify before use.

1. Opportunity was designed to last 90 Mars days and drive about 1,000 m (1,100 yards). It worked for more than 14 years and drove more than 45 km (28 miles). [A, NASA release Feb 13, 2019, as reproduced by Spaceflight Now and others]
2. Its last communication was on June 10, 2018 (Sol 5111), during a global dust storm. [A]
3. NASA declared the mission complete on Feb 13, 2019, after more than 1,000 commands got no reply. [A/B]
4. On May 30, 2018, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter spotted a dust storm heading toward Opportunity. By June 10 the storm covered more than 41 million km2 (15.8 million square miles), "about the area of North America and Russia combined". [B, NPR quoting NASA]
5. The storm "effectively turned day into night" over Opportunity, which sat in Perseverance Valley. [B, NPR quoting NASA]
6. Dust opacity is measured by "tau". Higher tau means less sunlight reaches a solar-powered rover. Average tau at Opportunity's site is 0.5. At the 2018 peak it was 10.8. [A, JPL Education, Feb 13, 2019]
7. The 2018 storm blocked sunlight for about four months. Only the top of Olympus Mons peeked above the dust. [A, JPL Education]
8. Opportunity had survived a global dust storm in 2007. This one was far worse. [B, NPR]
9. Engineers said that with so little power, the rover may not have been able to protect its electronics from the cold nights. [B, NPR]
10. Engineers hoped wind might blow the dust off the solar panels, so they sent commands "in the blind". [B, Spaceflight Now]
11. Curiosity is nuclear-powered, so dust storms do not cut its power. [B, NPR]
12. Opportunity landed in Meridiani Planum in January 2004. [B]
